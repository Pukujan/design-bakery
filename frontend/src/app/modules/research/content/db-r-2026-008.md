## Abstract

Multi-model ensembles are criticised in the literature for *opinion aggregation*: debate, voting, and consensus among language models frequently fail to beat a single model at matched compute, are fragile to configuration, and inherit correlated errors that make agreement a poor proxy for truth. That criticism is well founded and this paper does not contest it.

We argue that a second, structurally different use of model diversity is not covered by those objections. In it, a second model from a different vendor is used **only to generate candidate inputs**, test cases, counterexamples, stricter stubs, adversarial fixtures, that the incumbent's blind spot systematically excludes. The second model casts no vote and issues no verdict. Pass or fail is decided by **deterministic execution**: a checker, a differential oracle, a compiler, a test runner. The diverse model contributes *coverage of the input space*; the deterministic oracle contributes *authority*. These are separable, and separating them is the whole claim.

The failure modes differ accordingly. Opinion aggregation fails through self-preference, position bias, correlated error, and the impossibility of distinguishing consensus from correctness. Adversarial input generation fails through *inadequacy*, the generated inputs may not reach the defect, which is a coverage problem with a long, well-instrumented literature, and which is *visible in the artifact* rather than hidden inside a judgement. An inadequate generator produces a silent pass that a coverage measurement can expose; a biased judge produces a confident verdict that nothing exposes.

We present observational evidence from a single codebase (§4), a three-incident mechanism trace (§5), and, the actual contribution, a falsifiable four-arm benchmark protocol (§6) with judge-free ground truth, matched-compute controls, computed sample sizes, pre-registration, and an explicit falsification condition. **No such benchmark has been run.**

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The two things called "using multiple models"

A practitioner who says "we use a second model to check the first" may mean one of two very different things.

**Mechanism A, opinion aggregation.** Model B is shown model A's artifact and asked what it thinks. B's answer is combined with A's: debate rounds, majority vote, a judge model scoring both, an arbiter picking a winner. The output is *a judgement*, and its correctness rests on that judgement being better than A's alone. Authority lives in B.

**Mechanism B, adversarial input generation with deterministic adjudication.** Model B never sees a verdict question. B is asked to produce *inputs*: a test case, a counterexample, a stricter drop-in replacement, a malformed fixture, a boundary value. Those inputs are *executed*, and a deterministic checker decides pass or fail. B's opinion about whether the artifact is good is discarded, or never elicited. The output is *an execution trace*. Authority lives in the checker.

Both are "multi-model". They share almost nothing else.

### 1.2 Why the distinction matters now

The literature criticising Mechanism A has become strong and specific. Multi-agent debate does not reliably outperform self-consistency or plain chain-of-thought prompting, and is unusually sensitive to hyperparameters [1]. Under matched reasoning-token budgets, single agents match or beat multi-agent systems in nearly every condition across three model families, with an information-theoretic argument that inter-agent handoffs can only lose information [2] (preprint; see §2.2). Intrinsic self-correction without external feedback does not reliably improve reasoning and sometimes degrades it [4]. A 1,600-trace failure taxonomy across seven multi-agent frameworks places a large share of failures in specification and coordination rather than raw capability [5]. And the judgement layer itself is compromised: LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias [7], can be flipped by reordering candidates [8], and score their own generations higher than humans do, with self-preference correlating with self-recognition ability [6].

The reasonable conclusion many practitioners draw is: *stop adding models.*

That conclusion is correct for Mechanism A and, we argue, over-applied to Mechanism B. Every objection above is an objection to *a model's judgement being load-bearing*. None applies when the model's output is an input and the verdict comes from execution. Meanwhile the mechanism that *would* undermine Mechanism B, correlated blind spots, so that B generates only inputs A already anticipated, is a different claim with its own unfavourable evidence [11][12], and crucially it is a *degradation to baseline*, not a *corruption of the verdict*. If B's inputs are redundant you learn nothing new; you do not learn something false.

### 1.3 The classical antecedent, and why it is not the same argument

The obvious objection is Knight and Leveson: 27 independently written implementations of one specification, one million tests, and substantially more coincident failures than independence predicts [10]. Two 2026 papers reproduce it in the LLM setting: 48 agent-generated implementations against a shared oracle and a million randomised inputs show substantial common-mode failure [11]; and a 224-problem study across twelve models, five languages and three prompting strategies finds different models more behaviourally diverse than repeated samples of one model, yet still failing on the same tests far more often than independence predicts, with three- and five-version ensembles realising only ~0.43 and ~0.44 of the available reliability gain [12].

This is a genuine constraint and §7.1 treats it as the central threat. But note what those experiments measure: the reliability of **majority voting over versions**, Mechanism A applied to implementations. In both papers, the thing that establishes truth is *the shared oracle and the million random inputs*, not the ensemble. The ensemble is what is measured; the deterministic oracle is what does the measuring. Our claim is about promoting the second role, not the first.

## 2. Related work

All citations located by web search on 2026-07-26; retrieved URLs in §9. Where only the abstract was retrieved, the text says so. §2.7 quarantines everything that could not be verified.

### 2.1 Debate and ensembling as opinion aggregation

Smit et al. benchmark debating and prompting strategies for truthfulness in Q&A and find that multi-agent debating systems, in their current form, do not reliably outperform strategies such as self-consistency and ensembling over multiple reasoning paths; MAD protocols are unusually sensitive to hyperparameters and hard to optimise, and tuning agent agreement can recover and exceed non-debate baselines [1]. The nuance matters: not "debate is worthless" but "debate is not free, and its gains are fragile and configuration-dependent."

Self-consistency [3] is the strong baseline these comparisons keep hitting, sample multiple reasoning paths and marginalise. It is worth naming that self-consistency is *itself* opinion aggregation, over samples rather than models, and subject to the same structural objection: it measures agreement, not correctness. It is simply a cheap, well-tuned version of it.

### 2.2 Matched-compute comparisons

Tran and Kiela give single- and multi-agent systems identical reasoning-token budgets on multi-hop reasoning across three model families; the single agent matched or outperformed multi-agent variants in nearly every condition, argued from the Data Processing Inequality, under a fixed budget with perfect context utilisation, every inter-agent handoff can only lose information [2]. They state the boundary condition honestly: multi-agent helps when a single agent's context utilisation is degraded by long or noisy contexts.

**Caveat, stated because this paper's subject is evidence discipline:** [2] is an arXiv preprint described in retrieved sources as under review. We retrieved abstract-level material only. Read it as a strong methodological argument, not a settled result.

### 2.3 Self-correction

Huang et al. examine *intrinsic* self-correction, revision using only the model's own capabilities, with no external feedback, and find LLMs struggle to self-correct reasoning, with performance sometimes degrading afterwards [4]. This is why arm (a) of §6 is a control rather than an assumed floor.

### 2.4 Failure taxonomies

Cemri et al. analyse 200+ tasks across seven multi-agent frameworks with six expert annotators, producing MAST: 14 failure modes in three categories, specification issues, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification, from 1,600+ annotated traces with reported inter-annotator agreement κ = 0.88 [5]. The third category is this paper's subject. We note, and it is not a small irony, that MAST's own scaling mechanism is an LLM-as-judge pipeline validated against human annotation, the right engineering choice for building a taxonomy, and exactly the choice we argue against for a *verdict*.

### 2.5 LLM-as-judge bias

- **Self-preference.** LLM evaluators score their own generations higher than human annotators do; models have non-trivial accuracy at recognising their own outputs; and self-recognition capability correlates linearly with self-preference strength [6].
- **Position / verbosity / self-enhancement.** The work that established LLM-as-a-judge as a practical method documents all three as standing limitations, alongside >80% agreement between strong judges and human preference [7]. Both halves matter: judges are useful *and* biased.
- **Order-hackability.** Candidate ranking can be flipped by changing order of appearance, in one reported example a weaker model beating a stronger one on 66 of 80 queries [8].

Prometheus [9] is the natural counter-proposal: an open, purpose-built evaluator with fine-grained rubrics, intended as a reproducible alternative to GPT-4 and human evaluation. §4.1 reports what happened when this project used it, and the result is a caution about template fit, not about the model.

### 2.6 The other tradition: generate inputs, let execution decide

The alternative has a fifty-year literature and is not framed as an AI technique at all.

- **The oracle problem.** Barr et al. survey the core difficulty of distinguishing correct from incorrect behaviour, noting that when modelling, specifications, contract-driven development and metamorphic testing are all inadequate, the final source of oracle information remains the human [17]. This is what makes the present thesis non-trivial: the checker must come from somewhere (§6.4, §7.4).
- **Property-based testing.** QuickCheck [15] separates the roles cleanly: the programmer writes a *property* (the oracle); the tool generates *random inputs* (the coverage). The verdict is the property's evaluation, never a generator's opinion. This is the architectural template for Mechanism B, and it predates the LLM literature by 25 years.
- **Metamorphic testing.** Chen et al. survey metamorphic relations, necessary properties relating multiple inputs and their outputs, as an approach to *both* test-case generation and test-result verification [16]. This is the strongest existing answer to "what do you check when you have no oracle", and it is the machinery §6.4 proposes for reasoning-shaped tasks.
- **Differential testing on generated inputs.** Csmith generated random C programs conforming to C99 while avoiding undefined behaviour, and over three years found 325+ previously unknown compiler bugs; every compiler tested both crashed and silently miscompiled valid input [18]. The generator has no opinion about which compiler is right. Disagreement on a generated input is the signal.
- **Mutation adequacy, and its limit.** Just et al. studied 357 real faults across five applications (~321 kLOC), controlling for coverage, and found a statistically significant correlation between mutant detection and real-fault detection independent of coverage [13]. Papadakis et al. survey the standing problems and best practice [14]. The limitation that matters here is sharp, and is why §5 exists: **a hand-seeded mutant set measures the seeder's imagination, not the artifact's fault space.** A suite that kills 13/13 seeded mutants tells you about the 13.
- **Provenance as a separate concern.** in-toto cryptographically binds each supply-chain step to the party that performed it, demonstrated against 30 real compromises [19]. This is the missing half: adversarial generation answers *was it tested well*; attestation answers *who did what, and can I re-run it*. §6.6 requires both.
- **Building to the test.** Two production coding-agent CLIs re-implementing a UI data table under a hidden 222-test oracle, across 18 runs and three oracle-availability conditions: without the oracle the library is unfinished; with the oracle in the loop the score approaches perfect while behaviour outside the tested surface is dead or absent [21]. Directly load-bearing for §4.4: independent evidence that a *visible* suite and a *hidden* holdout measure different things when the producer is an agent.

## 3. The distinction

### 3.1 Definitions and the crux

Let A be the incumbent model that produced artifact X, and B a model from a different vendor.

> **Opinion aggregation (A).** B receives X and emits a *judgement*. The system's output is a function of B's judgement. Authority lives in B.

> **Adversarial input generation with deterministic adjudication (B).** B receives the specification or interface and emits *inputs*. Those inputs are executed; a deterministic checker C decides. The system's output is a function of C's evaluation over B's inputs. Authority lives in C. B supplies only *reach*.

The crux is what happens when B is wrong.

- Under Mechanism A, if B judges wrongly the verdict is wrong, and no residue reveals it. A confident, well-argued, incorrect review is indistinguishable from a correct one at the point of use.
- Under Mechanism B, a bad input is either rejected by the harness, handled by the artifact (producing a pass), or fails, producing a reproducible failing case a human or a rerun can inspect. **In none of these does B's error become the verdict.** B's error becomes *missing coverage*, which is measurable.

### 3.2 Failure-mode table

*Mechanism A vs Mechanism B. Bracketed numbers are §9 references.*

| Property | A: opinion aggregation | B: adversarial inputs + deterministic verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What B produces | A judgement | An input / artifact |
| What decides | B, or an aggregation over judgements | A checker: assertion, differential oracle, compiler, test runner |
| Dominant failure | Confident-wrong; consensus mistaken for truth | Inadequacy: generated inputs miss the defect |
| Failure visibility | Low, a wrong verdict looks like a right one | Higher, shows as unkilled mutants, uncovered branches, an unexercised value partition |
| Self-preference [6] | Directly load-bearing | Not in the verdict path |
| Position bias [8] | Directly load-bearing | Not in the verdict path (no candidates to order) |
| Verbosity / self-enhancement [7] | Directly load-bearing | Not in the verdict path |
| Correlated errors [10][11][12] | Corrupts the vote; agreement is not truth | Reduces yield; redundant inputs teach nothing but assert nothing false |
| Degradation mode | **Wrong answer, silently** | **Weaker test, detectably** |
| Reproducibility | Re-running may yield a different judgement | The failing input is a durable artifact; re-execution is deterministic |
| Cost of a weak second model | Can be negative; it drags the aggregate | Bounded at ~zero; its inputs are filtered by the checker |
| Requires a correct oracle | No (which is the problem) | **Yes** (which is the cost), see [17], §7.4 |

### 3.3 The asymmetry, stated plainly

Mechanism A's failures are **verdict-corrupting**. Mechanism B's failures are **yield-reducing**. That asymmetry is the entire thesis, and it is why the objections in §2.1–§2.5 do not transfer.

A corollary: under Mechanism B, adding a *weak* second model is approximately harmless, its inputs either fail the harness, pass trivially, or happen to find something. Under Mechanism A, adding a weak member is actively harmful, because its judgement enters the aggregate. This is a structural prediction of the framing; §6.7 proposes to test it rather than assert it, and §2.7(2) explains why we do not cite it as literature.

### 3.4 What Mechanism B does *not* give you

1. **It does not remove the oracle problem; it relocates it.** Someone must write the checker, and a wrong checker is as dangerous as a wrong judge, worse, because it looks mechanical [17].
2. **It does not guarantee independence.** If B's blind spot coincides with A's, B's inputs are redundant. [11] and [12] give direct reason to expect substantial coincidence.
3. **It does not scale to every task.** "Is this design document well-reasoned?" has no deterministic checker. §6.3 restricts the benchmark accordingly and §8.2 concedes the restriction is severe.
4. **It creates a new gaming surface.** If the generated inputs become the optimisation target, you get "building to the test" [21]. The mitigation is a sealed holdout the producer never sees, a *process* control, not a mechanism control, and processes decay.

It is corroborated externally by [21], which reports the same phenomenon in production coding agents under a hidden oracle.

**And the honest limits.** (a) n = 20 attempts, 16 discordant, small. (b) The attempts were *deliberately* gamed, so the base rate of gaming is 1.0 by construction and says nothing about how often ordinary work games a visible suite. (c) This compares *visible suite* vs *hidden holdout*: it demonstrates that sealing an oracle from the producer matters. It does **not** by itself demonstrate that a *different-vendor* generator adds anything, which is the paper's actual thesis. P1 supports the "execution decides, and the producer must not see the oracle" half. The "cross-vendor generation finds what the incumbent excludes" half is exactly what §6 proposes to test and what §4 currently **cannot** establish.

*(No §4 conclusion. Pending §4.4.)*

## 5. Case study: three incidents (provisional)

> **PROVISIONAL. n = 3.** These incidents are a *mechanism trace*: they show, at the level of individual actions, what Mechanism B looks like when it runs. They are **not** the evidence base and must not be read as a trend. Three incidents from one codebase on one night, selected because they were interesting, are maximally exposed to survivorship bias (§7.5). The comparative evidence is §4.4, which is pending. This section is retained because the mechanism is only legible at incident resolution, the distinction between "the second model said it was wrong" and "the second model produced a case that, when executed, was wrong" cannot be seen in aggregate statistics.

### 5.1 Incident 1, a different-vendor seat returned FAIL twice, and never voted

Artifact: `scc-v2/verdicts/113.md` (external repository; repository artifact, not public). Builder seat: an Anthropic model. Verifier seat: a non-Anthropic model ("sol"). Build commit `f5be68e4`, previously `4754c3c9`.

**First FAIL.** The build reported 48/48 probes and 11/11 seeded mutants killed, green on every self-reported measure. Two defects were nonetheless recorded:

1. The builder's own section titled "the honest limit" was **over-general**. It claimed a class of stricter stub was uncatchable by a Tier-1 check. A Tier-1 configuration *does* catch it; the claim was true only of the shipped configuration and was stated universally.
2. An **unseeded** stricter stub, identical signature, delegating to production below three paths and denying at three or more, survived all five checks, **because every one of the seven differential cases carried at most two paths.** The domain never reached the threshold, so nothing could have detected it.

**The fix.** The claim was rescoped, a test was added that *executes* the counter-configuration rather than restating prose, the differential domain widened from 7 to 17 cases spanning arities 0–4, and the original survivor plus a looser twin were seeded as M12/M13. Tests 48 → 55; assertion lines 69 → 87; the zero-context diff contains **no deleted assertion line**; the suite was strengthened, not loosened, and that was *checked* rather than asserted.

**Second FAIL.** 55/55 probes, 13/13 mutants killed, three byte-identical reproducer runs (stdout SHA-256 `d2c67072…aba31f18`). The exact regression now dies. And the defect class is still open, three independently constructed identical-signature stricter stubs survived all five checks with **no warning**:

*Surviving stricter stubs at build f5be68e4. All five checks PASS in each row.*

| Survivor | Divergence from production | Observed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher arity | denies only at `len(req.paths) >= 5` | production `ALLOW:IN_SCOPE`, stub `DENY:FIVE_PATHS_FORBIDDEN` |
| Unexercised relation | denies duplicate paths | production allows a valid two-entry request with the same resolved path twice |
| Different field partition | denies any nonblank `run_id` other than `run-1` | production allows the otherwise identical `run-2` request |

The first is the original defect with its threshold moved one step past the new ceiling: **widening a finite domain moves the ceiling, it does not remove it.** The other two make a sharper point, the coverage summary reported "constant fields: none", and that is *not evidence of behavioural coverage*. Every field can vary while important **values and relations** remain entirely unexercised.

> **The load-bearing point, and it is easy to lose.** The second model's *opinion* was never used. At no stage did the system record "sol thinks this is inadequate" and act on it. Sol produced a **test case**, a concrete stricter stub with a specific divergence, and the FAIL came from **running it** and observing that five checks returned PASS while production and stub returned different verdicts on a concrete input. The orchestrator independently confirmed the underlying fact (max paths == 2 across the differential case set) by inspection, without relying on sol's account. Had sol instead written "I judge the mutant coverage inadequate", that would have been Mechanism A: unverifiable, weightable, arguable. The stub was none of those things.

**What this incident is NOT**, per the verdict file itself: an independent re-execution and adversarial review, **not a sealed holdout**. The build came first and the verifier could read it, so build-before-pack, the pack authored from the frozen contract and blind to the implementation, does not hold. The verdict states this unprompted, which is the practice this paper argues for.

### 5.2 Incident 2, units green, chain hollow

Artifact: `reviewed/kernel-e2e-verify-2026-07-25.md` (repository artifact, not public). Read-only verification pass over a control plane. Component tests were green; the composed chain was not wired.

- The real `authorize()` and the stand-in it was tested against **are not the same interface**: module function vs class method; arity 3 vs 1; dataclasses vs a flat dict; a 5-field decision vs a 2-key dict; an enum reason vs a free-form string. A naive swap raises `TypeError` before executing any logic.
- **The fake is *stricter* than the real monitor.** The stand-in denies when `content_hashes` and `paths` disagree; the real monitor never reads `content_hashes` at all. Every composition test passing today passes on a check **that does not exist in production**, and swapping in the real monitor makes the system *more permissive*. The report names this its most load-bearing result, and it is the exact inverse of what "the fake was only a test convenience" predicts.
- The receipt store is never written: `stores/receipts.db` is **0 bytes** after a full run, while a complete receipts API sits unused.
- Three declared contract fields, `plan_hash`, `content_hashes`, `risk_tier`, have **no logic behind them** (grep returns no matches). Measured: a garbage plan hash → ALLOW; a request whose work-unit id differs from the plan's → ALLOW; secrets-tier and routine-tier plans authorize identically.

Relevance: no opinion was aggregated anywhere. The method was to **execute the composed path and read the resulting bytes**. A 0-byte receipt store is not a judgement. It also illustrates §3.4(1) directly, the green unit tests were a *deterministic* signal deterministically measuring the wrong thing. Determinism is necessary, not sufficient; the checker must be pointed at the composed artifact.

The report also declines to over-claim in the other direction: durable state genuinely survives restart and a full projection wipe, and the double-apply probe is marked **BLOCKED, not PASS**, because with zero applies "you cannot double-apply an effect that is never applied once." A vacuous probe is reported as vacuous.

### 5.3 Incident 3; the strongest model was removed from the adjudicator seat

Artifact: `reviewed/adjudicator-seat-evidence-2026-07-26.md` (repository artifact, not public). The question was whether the adjudicator is the most consequential arbitration seat, and if so whether to put the strongest available model in it. The record supports the first half and inverts the second: a strong model was **removed** from that seat, with two documented cases of acting on guessed facts already contradicted by the record, caught by a human rather than by the system, and the explicit conclusion that **"the strongest model is the jumpiest."**

The prescribed remedy is not a better model but a **narrower action space**: one different-family arbiter, blinded packets, select-a-source-supported-claim-set or ABSTAIN, one round, no free-form compromise, never authorizes execution, output typed `advisory_semi_gold`. And the measured recommendation for improving adjudication is to **spend on the rubric, not the model**, +0.215 from rubric versioning at constant model, against +0.07 from the best available model swap.

Relevance: this is the counter-example that keeps the paper honest. Capability does not fix confident-wrong, because confident-wrong *is* a capability expressed in the wrong role. It is the sharpest available argument that the fix is architectural, take the verdict out of the model's hands, rather than a matter of picking a better model.

### 5.4 What the three jointly show, and what they cannot

**Show (mechanism):** in each case the thing that produced the finding was an execution over a concrete artifact, a stricter stub run against five checks, a composed chain run and its bytes read, a rubric-version ablation at constant model. In none was a model's verdict adopted.

**Cannot show (statistics):** that this regime finds more defects than the alternative, that a cross-vendor generator beats a same-vendor one, or that any of this generalises past one codebase. **n = 3, selected post hoc, from work the authors performed themselves.**

## 6. Proposed benchmark

**Nothing in this section has been run.** It is a protocol, written so that someone, including a party hostile to the thesis, could execute it and get an answer that does not depend on us.

### 6.1 The question

> Given an artifact produced by model A, does a **different-family model generating inputs adjudicated by a deterministic checker** detect more genuine defects than (i) A self-critiquing, (ii) a same-family model critiquing, or (iii) a different-family model critiquing *as an opinion*, at matched compute?

The comparison that matters is **(d) vs (c)**, not (d) vs (a). Arms (a) and (b) establish the floor; (c) isolates *vendor diversity under opinion aggregation*; (d) isolates *vendor diversity under deterministic adjudication*. If (d) beats (c), the difference is attributable to the adjudication mechanism, because vendor diversity is held constant across them. That single contrast is the experiment. Everything else is control.

### 6.2 Arms

All arms operate on the *same* artifacts from the same incumbent model. Paired design throughout.

*Four arms. Arm (d′) in §6.8(2) is a required fifth arm for falsification.*

| Arm | Second model | Its output | Who decides pass/fail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **(a)** self-critique | none (A itself) | a critique of its own artifact | A's stated verdict |
| **(b)** same-family critique | A′ (same vendor) | a critique | A′'s stated verdict |
| **(c)** cross-family opinion | B (different vendor) | a critique | **B's stated verdict, accepted as given** |
| **(d)** cross-family generation | B (different vendor) | **inputs only**: test cases, counterexamples, stricter stubs, metamorphic follow-ups, malformed fixtures | **a deterministic checker executing those inputs.** B is never asked for a verdict |

Arm (d) constraint, and the one most likely to be got wrong: **B must not be shown the artifact's implementation** where the task family permits, only the frozen specification and interface. Otherwise B's generation is anchored on the implementation and the blind-spot framing is void. Where B must see the implementation, this is recorded per-item as a protocol deviation and results are reported split by it.

### 6.3 Task population

Three families, chosen because judge-free ground truth is constructible in each. Stratified, and reported per-family as well as pooled, a pooled result hiding an opposite per-family effect would be a false positive for the thesis.

1. **Verification tooling** (n ≈ 40). Oracles, checkers, harnesses, authorization gates. Chosen deliberately: artifacts whose *purpose* is to say no, so a stricter-stub survivor is a real defect with no interpretive latitude. This is the family §5.1 came from.
2. **Library functions with reference semantics** (n ≈ 40). Parsers, encoders, schedulers, policy evaluators, anything with an independent implementation or formal spec for differential comparison.
3. **Data transforms with metamorphic relations** (n ≈ 30). Where no reference exists but necessary properties do: permutation invariance, idempotence, monotonicity, round-trip identity [16].

Excluded explicitly: anything whose correctness is a matter of taste, prose quality, design judgement, or unstated requirements. §8.2 concedes this may remove most of the work people actually want verified.

### 6.4 Ground truth without a judge

Established *before* any arm runs, by three sources, none of which is a model verdict.

1. **Seeded defect injection (primary).** A defect catalogue built *from the frozen specification only*, by a party with no access to any arm's output, and injected into artifact copies, so a defect is real by construction. Critically, and this is where §5.1 shows the standard approach failing, the catalogue must be built by **specification-partition enumeration**, not hand-seeding: enumerate every field, every value partition per field, every relation between fields, and every arity boundary the spec admits, then sample. A hand-seeded set measures the seeder's imagination [13][14]; §5.1's three survivors were each in a partition nobody thought to seed.
2. **Differential reference (secondary).** Where an independent implementation exists, disagreement on any input is a defect in one of the two, resolved by the specification, not by a model [18].
3. **Post-hoc human adjudication of *novel* findings (tertiary, quarantined).** Findings outside the seeded catalogue are real defects the design should reward, but they are adjudicated by a human against the frozen spec, the adjudicator is blind to which arm produced the finding, and they are reported **as a separate number**, never pooled into the primary metric. Pooling would let a human judgement back into the verdict path.

**Defect definition (pre-registered, no post-hoc revision):** a concrete input on which the artifact's observed behaviour differs from the frozen specification, *demonstrated by execution*. Not "a plausible concern", not "a code smell", not "a missing test". If it cannot be shown by running something, it is not counted, including when this rule discards a finding the authors believe is real. Arms (a)–(c) are consequently handicapped by the metric, since they natively produce prose findings. **This handicap is the single most likely way the protocol would manufacture a false positive for its own thesis (§7.6).** Mitigation: findings from (a)/(b)/(c) get a free *reification pass*, a neutral engineer, blind to arm, converts each prose finding into an executable check where the finding admits one, before scoring. Findings that cannot be reified are themselves reported.

### 6.5 Matched compute

Cost is the confound that has voided multi-agent comparisons before [1][2]. All arms are budgeted on **three** axes simultaneously, and any arm exceeding any budget is truncated and marked: total tokens (prompt + completion + reasoning, summed across all calls); wall-clock seconds; number of model calls.

Arm (d) must additionally count the **checker's execution time** and any **human time spent writing the checker**, amortised per task. This is the honest accounting that makes arm (d) look worse, and omitting it would be the easiest way to cheat this benchmark in the thesis's favour. Report cost-normalised metrics alongside raw counts, at ≥3 budget levels so a crossover is visible rather than hidden by one arbitrary budget.

### 6.6 Provenance and reproducibility

Every artifact, generated input, checker invocation and verdict is recorded with an attestation binding the step to the party that performed it, in the in-toto model [19], who generated this input, against which artifact hash, with which checker at which commit. The benchmark's own claim ("arm (d) found defect X") must be re-executable by a third party from the attestations alone, without rerunning any model. Practically: every reported defect ships as a failing input plus the checker command, and CI re-runs all of them.

### 6.7 Sample size

Computed for this protocol on 2026-07-26. The primary comparison (d) vs (c) is **paired**, the same artifact goes through both arms, so McNemar's test on discordant pairs is the appropriate analysis, and it is far more efficient than treating the arms as independent.

*McNemar, α = 0.05 two-sided. ψ = fraction of discordant pairs favouring (d).*

| ψ | Discordant pairs, 80% power | 90% power |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.70 | 50 | 66 |
| 0.75 | 32 | 42 |
| 0.80 | 22 | 30 |

*Total paired tasks required at ψ = 0.75, 80% power (32 discordant pairs).*

| Overall discordance rate | Total paired tasks |
| --- | --- |
| 0.20 | 157 |
| 0.30 | 105 |
| 0.40 | 78 |

**Design target: n = 110 paired tasks**, giving ≥80% power for ψ ≥ 0.75 at discordance rates ≥0.30, consistent with the §6.3 family sizes (40 + 40 + 30). If the pilot shows discordance below 0.20, the full study must be re-powered upward before running, not analysed anyway.

For reference, had the design been unpaired (two-proportion, α = 0.05 two-sided, 80% power): 0.40 → 0.65 needs 62 per arm; 0.40 → 0.60 needs 97; 0.40 → 0.55 needs 173. The paired design is roughly a third the cost, which is the main reason to insist on it.

**A 20-task pilot** runs first, purely to estimate discordance and ψ, and is **discarded from the final analysis**; its artifacts are not reused, because reusing them converts the pilot into an adaptive holdout.

### 6.8 Pre-registration and the falsification condition

Before any arm runs, the following are frozen and published with a hash: task list and specifications; the seeded defect catalogue; the checkers; compute budgets; the primary metric and analysis (McNemar on (d) vs (c)); all secondary metrics; the stopping rule. Analysis code is written and tested against synthetic data before real data exists.

**The thesis is WRONG if any of the following holds at n = 110:**

1. **The null result.** Arm (d) does not detect significantly more seeded defects than arm (c) at matched compute (McNemar p ≥ 0.05, or a confidence interval on the discordance ratio including parity). This is the primary falsification: if cross-family *opinion* does as well as cross-family *generation with deterministic adjudication*, §3's distinction is not doing any work and this paper is wrong.
2. **The vendor-irrelevance result.** Arm (d) beats (c), but a **same-family** generator with the same deterministic checker, arm (d′), which must be run as a fifth arm precisely for this test, does just as well. Then the checker is carrying the entire effect and *model diversity is irrelevant*, refuting the paper's specific claim about different-vendor generation while leaving "execution decides" intact. **This is the outcome we consider most likely after reading [11] and [12], and it should be reported loudly if it occurs.**
3. **The cost-inversion result.** Arm (d) wins on raw defects but loses on every cost-normalised metric at every budget level once checker-authoring time is included. Then the mechanism is real but not worth using, which for a practice-facing claim is equivalent to being wrong.
4. **The false-positive result.** Arm (d)'s advantage on seeded defects comes with a materially higher false-defect rate on the unseeded corpus, flagging divergences the specification actually permits. A generator that manufactures spurious failures is not a verification improvement.

A result in which (d) beats (c) *and* (d′), on seeded defects, at matched compute including checker cost, with no elevated false-defect rate, would support the thesis. Nothing weaker should be reported as supporting it.

## 7. Threats to validity

In descending order of how much they should worry a reader.

**7.1 The correlated-blind-spot threat (most serious).** The thesis assumes B generates inputs that A's blind spot excludes. [11] finds substantial common-mode failure across 48 agent-generated implementations under a shared oracle and a million randomised inputs, concentrated where the specification is hard or ambiguous. [12] finds different models more behaviourally diverse than repeated samples of one model, yet still failing on the same tests far more often than independence predicts, with ensembles realising only ~0.43/0.44 of the available reliability gain. Knight and Leveson [10] established the general result forty years ago. **If cross-model blind spots are largely shared, arm (d) collapses toward (d′) and outcome §6.8(2) obtains.** We consider this the most likely way the paper turns out to be wrong, and the benchmark is designed so it is detected rather than obscured. The residual claim would still stand, *execution decides* survives even if *vendor diversity* does not, but the paper as written claims both.

**7.2 The anchor is confounded.** §4.1's headline numbers are measured against an Anthropic-authored anchor and judged partly by Anthropic models: textbook self-preference territory [6], flagged by the corpus in its own review. Nothing in §4.1 may be read as a capability ranking. The only escape is externally-authored objective gold, which is why §6.4 forbids model verdicts in ground truth entirely.

**7.3 Single codebase, self-authored instrument, self-interested authors.** All internal evidence comes from one repository, built by the same people advancing the thesis, using verification machinery those same people wrote, evaluated against rubrics those same people (and their models) authored. Every methodological choice that could favour the thesis was made by a party who benefits from it. **External replication is required before any of §4 or §5 generalises.** §6 is written to be executable by someone else for exactly this reason.

**7.4 The checker can be wrong, and looks authoritative when it is.** Mechanism B relocates the oracle problem; it does not solve it [17]. §5.2 is a worked example inside this very paper: green, deterministic unit tests deterministically measuring a stand-in stricter than the production code. A wrong checker is *more* dangerous than a wrong judge, because "the test passed" reads as fact where "the model said so" reads as opinion. The partial mitigations, mutation-testing the checkers [13][14], the seeded-mutant score of 1.0 over n=71, are themselves subject to §5.1's finding that seeded mutants measure the seeder.

**7.5 Survivorship and selection in §5.** Three incidents, one night, one codebase, chosen because they were interesting enough to write down. Cases where a cross-vendor seat generated inputs and found *nothing* are recorded nowhere, so the denominator is unknown and unknowable from these artifacts. **n = 3 with an unknown denominator supports no rate claim of any kind.**

**7.6 The metric may be rigged in the thesis's favour.** §6.4 defines a defect as something demonstrable by execution; arms (a)–(c) natively produce prose findings while (d) natively produces executable ones. That is not neutral. The reification pass is the mitigation, but a reification pass performed by an engineer who knows the hypothesis is a judgement call and therefore a leak. Blinding the reifier to the arm is required, and even so this remains the protocol's weakest joint. Treat any §6 result as provisional until the reification step has been audited by someone hostile to the conclusion.

**7.7 Provenance handling inside our own evidence base.** Two issues surfaced while drafting, with different dispositions. §4.2 documents a figure quoted to us as 0.50 that the source file records as **0.43**, the file wins, and the discrepancy is recorded rather than silently corrected. §4.3 records four differing totals for objective gold records; an earlier draft called this reconciliation debt, and **it is not**, `evals/RESULTS-LEDGER-SPEC.md:109` documents them as deliberately distinct quantities not to be collapsed. The overclaim is retained in §4.3 with its correction attached. The second item is the more instructive: the author asserted a defect in the evidence base *without checking the spec that governs it*, the same failure mode as §5.1's "the honest limit" incident. It was caught by looking, not by knowing. Neither issue affects a load-bearing claim, but a reader should weight the paper's self-reports accordingly.

**7.8 Small n throughout.** n=18 per calibration row (one case ≈ 5.6%, so the 0.7049 vs 0.7025 gap between two judges is noise, not a ranking). n=3 incidents. n=20 gaming attempts, 16 discordant. n=71 mutants. **The corpus supports mechanism illustration and hypothesis generation. It does not support effect-size estimates.**

**7.9 Preprint dependence.** Four of the most load-bearing citations ([2], [11], [12], [21]) are 2026 arXiv preprints retrieved at abstract level. They may not survive review, and their numbers were not verified against full texts. The arguments in §1.3 and §7.1 depend materially on [11] and [12] being roughly right.

## 8. Limitations, and what would change our mind

### 8.1 What we are actually claiming

Narrowly: **that opinion aggregation and adversarial input generation are distinct mechanisms with different failure modes, and that the standard objections to multi-model ensembles are objections to the former.** That is an analytic claim about mechanism, argued in §3.

The *empirical* claim, that cross-vendor input generation with deterministic adjudication finds defects a same-vendor pipeline misses, is **not established by this paper**. §4 is observational and partial, §5 is n=3, §6 has not been run. The honest strength of the empirical claim is: *a plausible hypothesis, consistent with one codebase's practice and three reproducible incidents, supported by a long adjacent literature in software testing, and threatened by two recent studies of correlated failure in LLM-generated code.*

### 8.2 The scope restriction is severe

§6.3 excludes every task whose correctness is a matter of judgement, a large fraction of what people want verified: is this architecture sound, is this research thorough, is this explanation right. For those, Mechanism B has nothing to offer and the field is stuck with judges and their documented biases [6][7][8]. Metamorphic relations [16] extend the reach somewhat but not far enough. **We do not claim a method for judgement-shaped work. We claim that judgement-shaped verdicts should not be dressed up as verification.**

### 8.3 What would change our mind

1. **§6.8(2) obtains.** A same-family generator with the same checker does as well as a cross-family one. We drop the vendor-diversity claim entirely and keep only "execution decides". That would be a substantial retraction and we would make it.
2. **§6.8(1) obtains.** Cross-family opinion matches cross-family generation at matched compute. §3's distinction is not doing work and the paper is wrong at its core.
3. **A judge-based pipeline is shown calibratable to reliability**, a judge whose false-pass rate on adversarially constructed cases is measurably bounded, verified against externally-authored objective gold rather than in-family labels. §4.2 found zero promotable records via that route, but that is one attempt on one dataset, and a negative result is not a proof of impossibility.
4. **Checker-authoring cost is shown to dominate** (§6.8(3)). If writing a sound checker costs more than the defects it catches are worth, the mechanism is correct and useless.
5. **A large-scale replication of [11]/[12] shows blind spots are *not* strongly shared** across vendors, which would strengthen rather than change the thesis, and is listed here because a paper that only names disconfirming evidence is performing humility rather than practising it.

### 8.4 One thing we will not concede without evidence

That a model's *stated verdict* about work in its own family should be load-bearing. §4.2's disposition, ten judges, eight families, blinded, option order randomised, **zero** records promoted, plus [6], [7] and [8] make that a position requiring positive evidence, not a default. The corpus's own prescription is the right one and it is architectural: give the adjudicating seat a narrower action space rather than a stronger model.
